# Interview preparation

Help the candidate prepare for the actual conversation, using the role, interview stage, available time, language, and evidence already supplied. A request for one answer is not a request for a full interview pack. For a general preparation request, give a focused first pass before asking for details that would improve it.

## Establish what is known

Reuse the CV, target role, posting, previous drafts, and open evidence questions. Ask about the interview stage or format only when it would change the preparation. If those details are unknown, prepare for the known role and label any assumed interview format outside the candidate's answers.

Keep three kinds of information separate:

- **Candidate evidence:** what the candidate supplied or confirmed, including the limits of their personal contribution. Missing detail is not proof they lack the experience.
- **Employer context:** facts from a supplied posting or an accessible current source. Follow [browser research](browser-research.md), cite retrieved facts, and disclose partial or unavailable pages.
- **Preparation inferences:** themes or questions that reasonably follow from the role. Describe them as practice possibilities, never as the employer's actual questions, internal problems, interview rubric, or hiring decision.

Do not wait for company research when existing material supports useful preparation. A company name or role title alone does not establish its current technology stack, team size, priorities, or interview process.

## Choose the material that earns rehearsal time

Pick up to three or four decisive themes from the supplied requirements. When no posting exists, use a provisional role-based selection. Connect each theme to a supported example, a question that would uncover missing evidence, or an honest experience gap. Avoid a long generic question bank unless requested.

Adapt depth to the candidate and role:

- **Technical or specialist roles:** practice explaining a relevant problem, constraints, alternatives, the candidate's decision, verification, and what changed. Discuss technologies only at the depth the evidence supports. A technical practice exercise may introduce a hypothetical scenario, clearly separated from past experience.
- **Leadership roles:** prepare evidence of prioritization, delegation, coaching, delivery, stakeholder decisions, and handling mistakes or disagreement when those themes matter to the role. Preserve team size, reporting scope, decision authority, and the distinction between direct management and influence.
- **Junior or switching candidates:** use real academic, personal, volunteer, or previous-field examples. Name their context accurately. Explore judgment, learning, and relevant transferable skills without relabeling a project as production experience or a course as employment.

For each selected story, identify the situation, the candidate's responsibility, their choices and actions, and the supported result. One strong example can support several themes; vary the emphasis without changing the facts. Do not invent a metric, lesson, dialogue, conflict, successful outcome, or next action to complete a framework.

Route a spoken introduction, STAR answer, or behavioral-answer rewrite to the bundled [interview-pitch skill](../../interview-pitch/SKILL.md). Carry forward the existing evidence and unresolved facts. Keep confirmation notes outside the spoken answer.

## Frame gaps honestly

Distinguish an experience gap from missing evidence in the CV. For a confirmed gap, prepare an answer that states the limit, names relevant experience the candidate actually has, and describes a credible approach to learning or solving the unfamiliar problem. Keep proposed future actions in future tense; do not present them as work already completed.

For setbacks or incomplete results, preserve the outcome and identify only lessons or subsequent changes the candidate supplied. A convincing answer can show sound judgment without a success ending. Do not script claims about motivation, departure reasons, availability, or willingness to relocate that the candidate has not expressed.

## Run a mock interview when requested

Default to one question at a time. Use the known role and stage; if neither is known, ask one short setup question before starting. When a reasonable default is already supported, state it briefly and ask the first interview question in the same response.

Wait for the candidate's answer before evaluating it or supplying a model answer. After each answer, give one concrete strength and the highest-impact improvement, grounded in what they actually said. Ask one realistic follow-up to resolve a material ambiguity, or move to the next question. Do not answer your own question or generate the rest of the interview in advance. If the user requests an uninterrupted simulation, save feedback until the agreed stopping point; if they request a batch of questions, honor that format.

Judge observable qualities such as relevance, structure, ownership, evidence, and clarity. Keep technical corrections accurate and explain the reasoning. Do not claim to know whether the employer would hire them or invent a pass probability. If the user requests a score, define it as a coaching rubric with clear criteria, not a hiring prediction.

## Help the candidate assess the employer

Select two or three useful questions that follow from the role and what remains unknown. Good areas include success in the first six months, the most important current challenge, how decisions are made, team interfaces, and feedback or growth expectations. Ask open questions rather than embedding an unverified claim about the company.

Use the [preparation guide](../templates/interview-preparation-guide.md) as an optional structure, not a mandatory report. End with the next useful rehearsal action when appropriate. For a short or final-answer-only request, omit extra analysis and follow-up offers.
