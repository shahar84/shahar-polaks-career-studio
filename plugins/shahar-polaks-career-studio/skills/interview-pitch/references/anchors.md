# Choosing credible evidence

An **anchor** is a specific fact that makes a claim understandable and credible.
Choose it for relevance to the role, accurate attribution, and clarity. A small,
directly owned outcome can be more useful than a famous employer or a large
company-wide number.

Ask: **What will the interviewer understand about this candidate from this
fact, and does the evidence support that conclusion?** A short sentence of
context is acceptable when it explains the candidate's contribution.

## Evidence types

These are options, not a prestige ranking:

- **Personal ownership:** A responsibility, decision, launch, system, or process
  the candidate actually owned. Specify the boundary when ownership was shared.
- **Concrete outcome:** A completed migration, reduced recurring failure,
  delivered feature, improved handoff, or other supported change. Qualitative
  outcomes are valid; do not invent a measurement to strengthen them.
- **Scale or complexity:** Verified users, team size, stakeholder scope,
  constraints, or before-and-after measurements. Preserve units, dates, and the
  distinction between personal, team, and company scope.
- **Position of trust:** A genuine leadership responsibility, first employee
  role, mentorship duty, or selection for a defined task. Do not convert informal
  influence into an official title.
- **Transferable experience:** A project, operational responsibility, customer
  problem, or learning that is relevant to the target role. Mark academic,
  volunteer, personal, and paid professional work accurately.
- **Company context:** A recognizable employer, acquisition, funding round, or
  product can supply context when verified and relevant. It does not establish
  the candidate's impact, dates of employment, or role in that event.

## Attribution and timing

Use "I" for the candidate's actions and "we" for a team's work when their role is
clear. Use "the company" for company-wide outcomes. Do not turn the company's
headcount into the number of direct reports, fundraising into revenue, or a
product's total audience into the candidate's personal reach.

For a later acquisition or milestone, check whether the candidate was employed
at the time before implying they participated. If it occurred after departure
and belongs in the pitch, say so clearly. Avoid a milestone entirely when it
adds prestige but no useful understanding of the candidate.

Treat dated rankings, current distribution claims, awards, and superlatives as
claims requiring appropriate support. If their meaning is ambiguous, omit them
from the usable draft and ask one focused question only when material.

## Selection rules

- Keep one clear evidence point per beat when possible. Add a second only when
  it explains the candidate's role or the outcome.
- Select for the actual responsibilities of the target role. Do not assume all
  interviewers in an industry respect the same logos or status markers.
- Include technical specificity when it makes fit legible, without giving a
  stack inventory or implementation lecture.
- Keep the narrative coherent. Omit irrelevant roles without changing the order
  of events or concealing a transition that matters to the answer.
- For juniors and career switchers, a bounded project and a clear personal
  contribution are useful evidence. No famous employer, big metric, or audience
  is required.
- When evidence is sparse, write a modest truthful answer. Stop asking for more
  facts once there is enough to fulfill the requested introduction.
