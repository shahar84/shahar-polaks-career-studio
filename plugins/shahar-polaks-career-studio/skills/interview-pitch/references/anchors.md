# Anchor taxonomy

An **anchor** is the recognizable or credible fact a claim rides on — the part the
listener can grasp quickly without a long explanation. The whole pitch is a chain
of anchors. A claim without an anchor is usually filler; cut it or find its
clearest truthful hook.

The test for any anchor: **would a smart outsider "get" why it's impressive
without you explaining?** If yes, it's an anchor. If you'd need a sentence of
context, it's too weak — swap it for a bigger one or drop the point.

## Anchor types (strongest first)

1. **Household names.** A company, product, person, or institution everyone knows.
   *Acquired by Google. Met Obama. In every iPhone. Represented Israel at the UN.*
   The name carries all the weight; you add nothing.

2. **Universally understood status.** A one-word outcome the listener instantly
   ranks. *Unicorn. IPO. Acquired. Sold. Bestseller. #1.*

3. **Scale numbers that need no unit-education.** Numbers whose magnitude is
   obvious. *$100M. 140 people. 50 → 50,000 customers. 1,000x in five years.* Ratios
   and multipliers ("1,000x", "50 → 50,000") often hit harder than raw figures
   because the growth is visible in the number itself.

4. **Recognizable position of trust or firstness.** *First employee. Founder.
   Led R&D. Only person who…* Pair with a household-name or status anchor so the
   listener computes your role in the big outcome (first employee + acquired = you
   were early at something huge).

5. **Concrete, verifiable scope or outcome.** When household names and huge
   numbers are absent, use a fact that still makes responsibility legible:
   *owned a critical launch, coordinated five stakeholders, reduced a repeatable
   failure mode, supported a complex customer migration, or became the go-to person
   for a defined problem.* Do not pretend these are bigger than they are; clarity
   and specificity are the anchor.

## Rules for using anchors

- **One anchor per beat is enough.** Don't stack three weak anchors to fake a
  strong one. One Google beats three obscure logos.

- **Stack anchors only when each is independently strong.** The example pitch
  stacks UN + Obama + "every iPhone" + Google in one breath — it works *because
  each one lands on its own.*

- **Prefer the anchor the listener respects, not the one you're proudest of.** The
  hardest thing you did may have no external anchor. The pitch rewards
  recognizability, not personal difficulty. Save the war stories for when they ask.

- **If a stop in the career has no clear anchor, it probably doesn't belong in the
  pitch.** This is the selection filter in action. For career switchers and early
  careers, a concrete transferable responsibility can be enough when it supports
  the target role.

- **Tune anchors to the audience.** When you know the target company/role,
  lead with the anchors that listener will respect most (a fintech interviewer
  weights "IPO" and "$100M"; a mission-driven org weights "UN" and the impact
  framing). Same facts, reordered for the room.
